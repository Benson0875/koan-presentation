# Group 2 · Full English speaking script

This is a rehearsal draft for the 16-page [presentation](index.html). The page numbers match the website. The wording is intentionally simple and spoken, so each presenter can use it directly or shorten it after practice. Text in square brackets is an action, not something to say aloud. The two films are separate from the speaking time.

## Presenter 1 · Pages 1–4 · About 3½ minutes of speaking

### Page 1 · Opening

Hello, everyone. We are Group 2. Today our report is about two koans: *One Who Is Never Hungry* and *One Who Is Not Sick*. We will use the same order for both. First, we will read the passage from our course handout. Next, we will show a short film set in everyday life. Finally, we will explain what we noticed in the film and how we connect it to the koan. Our films are our own readings. They give us a way into the questions, but they do not claim to explain every possible meaning. Let's begin with the first passage.

### Page 2 · One Who Is Never Hungry

[Read the passage on the slide slowly and clearly.]

Master BaiZhang addressed the assembly, “There is one who never eats yet never feels hungry, and there is one who eats all day yet never feels full.” The assembly was silent.

Two people appear in this very short passage. Their situations seem impossible if we only think about food. The person who keeps eating still does not feel full. The person who never eats does not feel hungry. Why might eating more fail to answer hunger? And why does the assembly stay silent? The passage does not give us a simple answer. We will focus first on the person who keeps eating. Our everyday story asks what happens when getting more of something gives us only a short moment of relief. We will return to the other person near the end.

### Page 3 · Before Film 1

Our first film is called *Three Days to Payday*. Alex likes a mobile game and has spent a lot of money on it. With three days left before payday, he is eating cup noodles for lunch. His coworker Jamie also plays the game, but their experiences are different. While you watch, notice two things: what Alex says he wants, and what he seems afraid of losing.

[Play Film 1. After it ends, move to page 4.]

### Page 4 · A win still feels empty

Alex begins with a win. He finally gets the character he wanted. But almost at once, he thinks about getting another copy. He says that thirty more pulls will be enough, and then he will stop. Jamie asks a simple question: where is the finish line? That question matters because Alex has moved the finish line before. At first, we might think he only wants a stronger character. Then he explains that he is afraid his guild may not want him on their team if he falls behind. Nobody has actually said that to him. Still, the fear makes each new release feel urgent. The prize is real, but the relief does not last.

**Handoff:** So what is Alex really trying to get when he keeps spending? [Presenter 2 takes over.]

## Presenter 2 · Pages 5–7 · About 3½ minutes of speaking

### Page 5 · The fear underneath

Alex tells Jamie, “I don't want to be the useless one.” That line changes how we see the cup noodles and the game. The problem is not only that a game keeps offering new things to buy. Alex is worried about his place among other players. He imagines being left behind, even though his guild has not said it will leave him out. This is why he keeps checking the group chat and looking for what he is missing. He says he used to look forward to playing. Now he spends more time checking than enjoying the game itself. The film starts with a funny contrast between a new game character and a cheap lunch. Under that image is a fear many people know: “What if I am not good enough to stay with this group?”

### Page 6 · Our reading of the first koan

This brings us back to BaiZhang's words. We connect Alex with the person who eats all day and never feels full. For Alex, another character promises that he will feel ready and accepted. But as soon as he gets one, a new goal appears. The game is not the only source of this problem. His fear keeps making “enough” move further away. We should be careful here: our film is one possible reading of one image in the koan. It does not mean that playing games is wrong, or that everyone who spends money on a hobby feels this way. Jamie plays too, and he is happy with casual matches. The question we want to ask is more personal: what are we feeding when we keep reaching for more but never feel full?

### Page 7 · One meal without checking

The ending matters because Jamie does not give Alex a big speech or order him to stop playing. When Alex asks if he should quit, Jamie asks, “Do you want to?” Alex says no. He wants the game to feel fun again. Jamie then asks what would be enough right now. Alex cannot answer for the rest of his life, but he can choose one meal without checking his phone. He places it face down and eats with Jamie. They even plan a casual match after work. This does not magically remove his fear. Tomorrow he may feel the same pressure. But he notices the difference between enjoying a game and using it to quiet a fear. That small moment is where his change can begin.

**Handoff:** Our second koan asks us to look again too, but this time at how we see other people. [Presenter 3 takes over.]

## Presenter 3 · Pages 8–10 · About 2¾ minutes of speaking

### Page 8 · One Who Is Not Sick

[Read only the course-handout excerpt shown on the slide.]

Master GuiShan asked Master DaoWu: “Where have you been?” Daowu replied, “Tending the sick.” “How many were sick?” “Some were sick, some were not.”

That is the part of the passage in our handout image. We are not adding later lines from another version. Guishan asks a direct question, and Daowu answers by putting people into two groups: sick and not sick. Sometimes a distinction like this is necessary. If someone has a fever, we need to know. But we also wondered what a label might fail to show. Does “not sick” mean that a person needs nothing? Can we know enough about somebody from one condition? Our next film puts those questions in a school clinic, where people arrive for different reasons.

### Page 9 · Before Film 2

The second film is called *Some Were Not*. Nora is a trainee nurse working with Dr. Lee. Three students come to the clinic: Sam, Evan, and Mia. Sam has a fever. Evan says he has a stomachache, but later admits that he made it up to avoid a test. Mia feels overwhelmed by a bad grade. Watch how Nora first sorts them and what makes her change her view.

[Play Film 2. After it ends, move to page 10.]

### Page 10 · Three students, one clinic

At first glance, Sam looks like the clearest patient. He has a fever and needs care and rest. Evan says his stomach hurts, but he later admits that he lied because he is afraid of the math test. That lie matters. It would be unfair to ignore it. Mia is deeply upset by a failed test and begins to speak as if the result defines her whole life. Her pain also matters. The film does not say these three situations are medically the same. It asks what Nora might miss if she only decides who is “sick” and who is “not sick.” Their needs are different, so a helpful response must be different for each one.

**Handoff:** What changes when Nora stops sorting people and starts listening? [Presenter 4 takes over.]

## Presenter 4 · Pages 11–13 · About 3½ minutes of speaking

### Page 11 · Nora's change in view

Nora starts by thinking about space in the clinic. Sam has a fever, so she understands why he is there. Evan lied about pain, and Mia is upset about a grade, so Nora is quick to question whether they belong. Dr. Lee does not tell her to forget the differences. He says, “Fear isn't a fever. But a thermometer can't tell us everything.” He then asks what they have not heard. Evan says he is afraid to fail again and be laughed at. Mia says people only ask about her score. These details were hidden behind the first labels. Nora notices what her quick judgment cost her. She says she was so busy deciding who belonged that she stopped listening. Her change begins with that honest admission.

### Page 12 · Our reading of the second koan

We connect this moment to the question raised by “Some were sick, some were not.” A label can be useful without describing the whole person. Sam's fever is real and needs medical care. Evan's lie is real too, and he should tell the truth. Mia's distress should be heard, even though a bad grade is not a fever. If we call all three students “sick” in the same way, we lose important differences. If we call Evan and Mia “not sick” and stop there, we may miss what they need. In our film, Nora answers, “Some were sick. Some weren't. All needed care.” That is our own line and our own response to the koan. It is not a line from the course handout.

### Page 13 · Listening leads to action

The film tries to show that listening leads to action. Sam is helped to rest while Dr. Lee contacts his family. Evan is asked to be honest with his teacher, and Nora offers to go with him. That is support with responsibility, not permission to keep lying. Mia is offered help from a counselor, and Nora offers to stay with her while they ask. That is a different kind of support. None of the students has every problem solved by the last scene. The fever is still real, Evan still has to face the test, and Mia still has to deal with her grade. But each person has a next step that responds to more than a first impression.

**Handoff:** Now we can put the two films side by side. [Presenter 5 takes over.]

## Presenter 5 · Pages 14–16 · About 4¼ minutes of speaking

### Page 14 · Compare the two stories

This table shows the path both films take. First, there is what we can see quickly. Alex buys more game characters. Sam has a fever. Evan lied. Mia failed a test. These facts are not false. But the next row asks what lies underneath. Alex is afraid of losing his place in the guild. Evan is afraid to fail and be laughed at. Mia begins to believe that one grade is her whole identity. The third row shows a change in response. Jamie asks Alex what enough would mean right now. Dr. Lee asks Nora what they have not heard. Neither question erases the facts. Instead, it helps the characters choose a better next step. Alex puts the phone down for one meal. Nora listens and offers different help to each student. [Pause and point across the last row.] Which hidden concern was easiest for you to miss?

[If the room responds, take one short answer. If it stays quiet, continue without pressure.]

### Page 15 · Two koans, different lessons

Even though the stories share this movement from first impression to a deeper question, the koans should stay distinct. The first one uses hunger and fullness. Our film explores how fear can make another reward feel necessary, even after a person has just won. But BaiZhang also speaks of a person who never eats and never feels hungry. Alex's story does not explain that figure. We leave it open. The second koan begins with the words “sick” and “not sick.” Our film explores how those words might guide a first response while leaving out the person in front of us. We keep Sam's fever, Evan's lie, and Mia's distress separate. The shared lesson is the habit of asking one more question before deciding that we understand everything.

### Page 16 · Closing question

We want to end with a question that reaches beyond the films. Think about Alex. It is easy to say that he only wants more. That is part of what we see, but it misses his fear of being left out. Think about Evan. He lied, and he needs to face that. But if we stop at “liar,” we may never hear why he was afraid to enter the classroom. The first thing we notice may be true. It may also be only one part of the story. In our two films, listening does not remove responsibility, illness, or difficult choices. It helps people respond to those things more carefully. So here is our question for the room: can we see the person beyond the label? [Pause. Take one or two brief responses if time allows.] Thank you for listening.

## Rehearsal notes

- Read the two handout passages exactly as written on pages 2 and 8. Page 8 is an excerpt; do not add later lines from another English rendering.
- Aim for **15–20 minutes of speaking**, plus the films. These page targets add up to about 17½ minutes, but actual pace will vary. Rehearse aloud with a timer once before presenting.
- If the report runs short, slow down at the two comparison questions and invite one brief response. If it runs long, skip the optional response on page 14 before cutting the analysis.
- Presenters 1 and 3 cue their films. Decide who clicks Next at each handoff before the presentation begins.
